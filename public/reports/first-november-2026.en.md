## General context

In the second October report, I settled on one idea: breadth was beginning to improve, but major technology leaders no longer participated as strongly. Two weeks later, the data advance that observation. **Rotation did not disappear; it became more evident.** The difficulty is that, while participation broadens in one part of the market, some companies contributing most to earnings growth continue to be punished.

Between October 16 and October 30, **SPY lost 0.99% and QQQ 2.72%**. Meanwhile, **RSP gained 1.97% and IWM 3.29%**. These moves differ enough that we should look beyond the S&P 500’s level. Focusing only on the capitalization-weighted index misses much of what is happening beneath the surface.

Interestingly, this divergence appears while companies continue reporting good results. According to FactSet, by October 30, with 60% of the S&P 500 reported, blended EPS growth reached **30.2% year over year** and revenue growth **13.4%**. Furthermore, **82%** had beaten earnings estimates.

At first glance, it is hard to call the season weak. But one nuance changes the observation considerably: the aggregate EPS surprise was only **1.9%**, well below the **7.5%** five-year average reported by FactSet. Many companies beat consensus, but by less than usual.

**Here is the question I want to explore in November: if earnings keep growing, why are prices not participating more broadly?**

My observation is that demonstrating growth is insufficient. Companies must also justify how much growth was already priced in, how much capital is needed to sustain it and how much ultimately becomes shareholder cash flow.

AI makes this situation clear. Computing demand grows daily, cloud revenue expands and new applications emerge, but investment budgets also keep increasing, converting earnings to cash flow is more demanding, and credit protection for some large technology companies is becoming more expensive. These facts are compatible. An industry can genuinely grow yet offer a poor relationship among expectations, the price paid and returns on capital for a period.

Monetary policy does not simplify the scenario. On October 28, the Fed held rates at **3.75–4.00%**, by ten votes to two. The ten-year Treasury finally fell below 5%, from **5.09% to 4.94%** between cutoffs, a decline of **15 basis points**, but VIX ended October 30 at **19.42**, with a notable increase that day. Falling yields are a welcome sign we had awaited, but do not automatically mean every market breathes easier; *why* they fall matters, alongside credit, growth expectations and liquidity.

The correction was not confined to US equities: between October 16 and October 30 closes, **GLD lost 6.87%, FXI 2.25%, EWJ 2.08%, BTC 5.23% and ETH 7.33%**. Each has its own reasons and catalysts; grouping them all together would be a mistake.

**I retain a selective market view rather than an automatic bullish thesis.**

Corporate growth and breadth improvement exist, but I want participation to persist, technology investment to generate reasonable returns and financial conditions to avoid transmitting pressure from large technology companies into other assets. Election seasonality favors certain historical scenarios; it does not resolve this November’s outcome.

### 2.3. Earnings, concentration and the cost of financing AI

#### Good earnings do not always guarantee good share-price reactions

Two things must be distinguished: **the results companies report and the expectations already priced into their shares**.

FactSet shows a strong earnings season, with 30.2% year-over-year S&P 500 growth. Positive surprises are nevertheless much smaller than usual: companies are doing well, but more was expected.

This is visible in the Stockpicking companies. Eleven have already reported; nine fell, and five of those declined more than the options market had anticipated through the *implied move*.

Not necessarily because they reported bad earnings, but because prices also reflect expectations, positioning and future forecasts.

Concentration is another important issue. According to Goldman Sachs, the ten largest S&P 500 companies represent approximately **39% of capitalization and 37% of expected index earnings**.

That is very high concentration. On one hand, the market depends heavily on a small group of companies. On the other, those companies also generate a substantial share of earnings.

High concentration alone does not establish that a correction is inevitable. Risk exists, but concentration does not tell us when or how that correction would occur.

#### AI grows, but what happens to returns on capital?

This could be one of the report’s most important themes.

AI demand continues growing. Goldman Sachs reports **54% year-over-year growth in aggregate Amazon, Alphabet and Microsoft cloud revenue** during the third quarter. OpenRouter also shows a considerable increase in processed token volume.

These are not merely expectations. Real activity and growing technology-infrastructure demand exist. However, the cost of sustaining that growth is also increasing.

According to consensus estimates compiled by BofA, five major companies—Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet, Meta and Oracle—could allocate approximately **US$1.11 trillion to capital investment in 2027**, equivalent to around 51% of projected S&P 500 capex.

This is where close observation is needed.

Goldman Sachs shows that those five companies’ aggregate free cash flow fell to approximately **−US$31 billion in the third quarter of 2026**. In a separate comparison of four large technology companies, quarterly capex reached approximately **US$201.9 billion**, against negative free cash flow near **US$26.4 billion**.

The samples reflect growing pressure on cash generation. Heavy investment is not necessarily bad. It may be exactly what a company needs to capture extraordinary market expansion.

**The difficulty is that share prices have often already priced in future returns that have not yet materialized.**

There are also favorable productivity indications. An EPB Research study shows accelerating productivity in particularly technology-intensive sectors. This is interesting, although it does not allow the entire improvement to be attributed exclusively to AI.

Rather than ask only whether AI will keep growing, I want to check how much growth becomes real, sustainable earnings and cash flow.

#### Technology versus healthcare: valuations changed

Another observation particularly caught my attention.

According to Duality Research, technology’s forward P/E was around **18.4 times**, while healthcare reached approximately **20 times**. Technology therefore traded at a multiple **1.6 points below healthcare**, contrasting with the usual perception that technology is always the most expensive sector.

Goldman Sachs also puts technology around 19x, close to the S&P 500’s valuation. Healthcare and consumer staples, by contrast, stand at high historical percentiles.

A lower relative valuation does not automatically mean technology is cheap, although it provides an indication. Earnings-estimate developments, sector composition and margin sustainability must be considered.

Financing costs also matter again for large technology companies. Morningstar shows that CDS spreads for some companies, including Meta, NVIDIA, Alphabet and Amazon, widened during 2026.

This does not establish solvency problems at those businesses. But it reminds us that **credit markets can begin questioning the financial conditions of an expansion still valued favorably by equity markets**.

#### Breadth improves, but not every signal tells the same story

Returning to breadth, so relevant in recent reports, Ryan Detrick shows a substantial recovery in S&P 500 companies trading above their 20- and 50-session averages.

Goldman Sachs, however, presents a less favorable picture when examining companies’ distance from 52-week highs. The Nasdaq McClellan Summation Index also reflects substantial deterioration over the observed period.

Although seemingly contradictory, these indicators measure different things. Breadth can recover after a correction while constituents have yet to regain longer trends or the index price has yet to reflect improvement. In other words, more companies may be recovering ground without prices having fully recovered.

**Ideally, I would check whether that recovery can persist, although depending on the investment methodology, that might be very late.**

Positioning also matters. BofA shows Bull & Bear at 7.1, while its private clients retain considerable equity exposure. Hedge funds, by contrast, have much lower net exposure. Each invests according to its horizon, risk and expectations.

BofA also estimates that, under certain conditions, systematic strategies could generate selling near **US$48 billion in a sideways market** and **US$79 billion in a bearish scenario**.

These are estimated scenarios, but help explain how movements can be amplified when volatility, liquidity and automatic position adjustments coincide.

The question for coming weeks is whether improved breadth persists in a more balanced market or large technology weakness eventually spreads to other sectors.

## Reading by asset

### 3.1. SPY — United States

**What happened**

SPY closed October 30 at **USD 755.72**, versus USD 763.25 at the previous cutoff, a decline of **0.99%**.

Companies within the market behaved very differently. While QQQ fell 2.72%, RSP gained 1.97% and IWM 3.29%.

This confirms what we had observed since October: leadership is being redistributed and companies are not all responding in the same way.

**What changed**

Earnings season continues to show significant profit growth. According to FactSet, blended S&P 500 EPS grows 30.2% year over year.

Estimate surprises are nevertheless below historical averages, while some of the largest technology companies have experienced substantial corrections.

Short-term breadth metrics also improved, but 52-week measures still show fragility.

Index concentration and large technology companies’ growing financing needs remain two important risks.

**What I expect**

I would like small companies and the equal-weight S&P 500 to keep recovering without broad deterioration among large technology companies. We also need to check whether corporate earnings keep supporting valuations and whether breadth recovery can last beyond a few sessions.

The scenario improves with broader participation, stabilizing financial conditions and continuing earnings growth. Conversely, deteriorating credit, a persistent volatility increase or a broader large-technology correction would require reviewing that outlook.

**Monitoring:** MA20/50/200 breadth, 52-week highs, RSP/SPY, IWM/SPY, QQQ/SPY, the Magnificent Seven, corporate earnings, CDS and VIX.

### 3.2. GLD — Gold

**What happened**

GLD went from **USD 382.01 to 355.78** between October 16 and October 30, falling **6.87%**.

The correction was substantial. As mentioned in the previous report, gold touched USD 4,000 per ounce. The metal nevertheless retains favorable structural arguments and naturally can experience significant declines.

**What changed**

Central-bank purchases remain a main structural-demand argument. According to the World Gold Council and J.P. Morgan, average official gold purchases increased considerably from mid-2022 relative to earlier years. China remains an important participant, according to its official reserve records.

Investment demand can temporarily diverge, however. Koyfin flow data show approximate **US$1.8 billion GLD outflows during October**.

Some bank outlooks, including J.P. Morgan’s, also warn of further downside pressure while real yields and monetary conditions remain demanding.

A favorable medium- and long-term outlook should not be confused with an immediate recovery signal.

**What I expect**

My observation remains one of long-term opportunity and short-term caution. Central-bank purchases provide important demand support, but do not guarantee a price floor.

To improve the outlook, I would like price stabilization, reduced GLD outflows and more favorable dollar and US real-yield conditions.

**Monitoring:** GLD price, spot gold, real TIPS yields, the dollar, GLD shares outstanding, investment flows and World Gold Council official purchases.

### 3.3. FXI — China

**What happened**

FXI ended the period at **USD 33.08**, versus USD 33.84 on October 16, a change of **−2.25%**.

CSI 300 charts show considerable technical oversold conditions and prolonged relative deterioration versus the S&P 500. We must distinguish two things: the CSI 300 and FXI do not represent exactly the same market.

The former mainly contains domestic Chinese shares, whereas FXI provides exposure to large Chinese companies listed in Hong Kong. An extreme CSI 300 technical signal cannot therefore be directly applied to expected FXI performance, but remains important.

**What changed**

October’s official manufacturing activity data were not particularly favorable either. China’s manufacturing PMI fell from 50.1 to **49.3**, with new orders at **49.4** and the composite output index at **49.7**.

These indicate an economy still struggling to regain dynamism.

Some technology segments have better prospects, but I do not consider that sufficient evidence of an established economic recovery.

**What I expect**

A rebound could naturally follow such pronounced technical deterioration. However, **oversold conditions do not necessarily mean a market bottom has formed**.

For a more favorable observation, I would like government incentives, global impact through AI developments, or at least improvements in economic activity, consumption, credit and corporate earnings, especially among companies actually held by FXI. Yuan developments will also matter.

**Monitoring:** FXI, Hang Seng China Enterprises, PMI, credit, consumption, PBoC stimulus, the yuan and earnings revisions.

### 3.4. EWJ — Japan

**What happened**

EWJ fell from **USD 94.88 to 92.91**, a decline of **2.08%**.

Japan retains interesting structural arguments, especially corporate reforms, buybacks, earnings growth and technology investment. One difference from other markets is important: yen movements can substantially change the return of dollar-traded EWJ.

**What changed**

Standard Chartered retains Japanese equities as a *Core Holding*, supported by expected twelve-month EPS growth of **16%** and a forward P/E near **15 times**. The bank expects performance roughly in line with global equities over the next six to twelve months. This does not forecast clear outperformance, but presents an additional opportunity that also provides diversification.

J.P. Morgan Asset Management also highlights Japanese reflation, meaning stimulus to prevent deflation, corporate-governance reforms, investment and increasing buybacks.

The Bank of Japan projects real GDP growth of **0.7%** for fiscal years 2026 and 2027, with inflation excluding fresh food at **2.5% and 2.6%**, respectively. Monetary normalization nevertheless introduces new valuation, bond-yield and yen risks.

Nomura Asset Management’s survey is also interesting: **66% of participants favored Japanese equities** and **53% expected yen appreciation**.

**What I expect**

I retain a constructive long-term outlook. Corporate reforms and earnings growth can keep helping the market, although financing costs and currency deserve attention.

A stronger yen could improve EWJ’s dollar return while hurting some exporters’ competitiveness.

I would like favorable earnings revisions, stable Japanese yields and monetary developments that avoid damaging valuations.

**Monitoring:** EWJ, TOPIX, USD/JPY, two- and ten-year JGBs, the Bank of Japan, EPS growth and corporate reforms.

### 3.5. BTC — Bitcoin

**What happened**

Bitcoin went from **USD 81,587.40 to 77,320.10**, a decline of **5.23%**.

Beyond price, there is an important flow change.

According to Farside, September produced around **US$2,647.7 million of net inflows into US Bitcoin spot ETFs**. October instead ended with approximate **US$515.9 million outflows**. October 30 alone concentrated outflows near **US$643.2 million**. October had previously been almost flat.

**What changed**

In early October, Citi raised its twelve-month Bitcoin target from **USD 82,000 to 113,000**, supported by a more favorable outlook for institutional activity and investment products.

J.P. Morgan had also identified recovery in several digital-asset financing and exposure channels during the third quarter. Those aggregate flows must nevertheless be distinguished from specific net spot-ETF inflows.

The late-October Binance theft also introduced custody, confidence and liquidity risks, particularly through lost trust.

Although Reuters later reported recovery or coverage of a large proportion of affected assets, the episode may continue affecting positioning and participant behavior.

We cannot attribute all October weakness to the incident, because some flow deterioration had already occurred.

**What I expect**

I do not treat twelve-month institutional forecasts as recovery confirmation, although they are conservative.

I would like sustained ETF-flow stabilization, improved liquidity conditions and reduced leverage vulnerability. Again, depending on the investment methodology, it might already be too late when this occurs.

Bitcoin’s institutionalization will continue advancing, but that does not require an immediate price recovery.

**Monitoring:** BTC/USD, Farside spot ETF flows, futures premiums, liquidations, leverage, liquidity and platform security.

### 3.6. ETH — Ethereum

**What happened**

Ethereum declined from **USD 2,423.29 to 2,245.58**, down **7.33%**.

US spot ETF flows also weakened. According to Farside aggregates, October recorded approximate **US$1,607.2 million outflows**, including **US$155.7 million** on October 30. Unlike BTC, ETH was already losing flows during October.

**What changed**

On October 19, J.P. Morgan announced **JPM Coin’s availability on Ethereum mainnet**, extending institutional usage beyond Base. This is a concrete advance in blockchain infrastructure adoption for payments and tokenized assets.

However, **using Ethereum as infrastructure does not necessarily create greater demand for ETH as a financial asset**. It does suggest increased confidence in its infrastructure.

J.P. Morgan states that Kinexys has processed more than **US$4 trillion in cumulative transactions**, with activity near **US$7 billion daily**. These figures cover all of Kinexys rather than only JPM Coin operations on Ethereum.

Citi had also raised its twelve-month ETH target from **USD 2,240 to 3,028** before late October’s deterioration. The challenge is determining how much economic value ETH captures as institutional infrastructure usage grows.

That requires examining fees, mainnet activity, layer-2 solutions, token issuance and burning.

**What I expect**

I retain a favorable infrastructure-development outlook with short-term price caution. In crypto, BTC remains king by capitalization, with substantial influence on its runner-up, ETH.

I would like ETFs for both cryptocurrencies to regain demand, network economic activity to keep growing and usage to generate verifiable value capture for ETH.

The ETH/BTC relationship will also matter.

**Monitoring:** ETH/USD, ETH/BTC, ETF flows, L1/L2 activity, fees, supply, ETH burning and tokenized assets.

One final relationship worth following is Japanese monetary normalization. Higher yen financing costs could change international leveraged positions, including some crypto exposures.

#### How we interpret these figures

The *implied move* is calculated from the midpoint cost of an **at-the-money straddle**, comprising a call and a put, divided by the stock’s reference price.

The actual move is compared using the first session incorporating earnings:

- For before-open (BMO) announcements, from the previous close to the announcement-day close.

- For after-close (AMC) announcements, from announcement-day close to the following session’s close.

A move beyond the implied range means **the reaction’s magnitude exceeded that estimated through options**. It does not necessarily mean the company beat or missed EPS estimates.

It is not a prediction of price direction either.

## Seasonality and calendar

### 4.1. November and midterm elections

We enter November with historically interesting seasonality for US equities.

Historical series shared by several analysts show **15 of the last 19 midterm-election Novembers finished positive**, with a median of approximately **2.23%**.

Another study shows that, since 1950, **all 19 six-month windows after October 26 in midterm-election years produced positive S&P 500 returns**, averaging around **15.4%**.

These are striking statistics, but must be interpreted correctly.

First, a six-month window is not equivalent to November’s result.

Second, a positive endpoint does not mean the period contained no substantial declines.

Third, these are relatively small historical samples under different economic, monetary and political conditions.

BofA’s comparison of 2026 with average midterm-election-year behavior is also interesting.

While the S&P 500 had gained approximately **10.7% in 2026 through October 30**, the average historical midterm-year path showed a gain near **0.9%** at that point in the calendar.

In other words, 2026 already differs substantially from the usual pattern.

Franklin Templeton also found that twelve-month post-election S&P 500 returns under Republican presidents were relatively similar across different congressional compositions in its historical sample.

I do not consider it reasonable to conclude that a particular election outcome guarantees better returns.

**Seasonality provides information about what happened under specific historical conditions rather than what must happen this November.**

## Possible paths

Improving US equities do not necessarily imply simultaneous recovery in gold, China, Japan and cryptocurrencies.

Each market has particular conditions, although all can be affected by US yields, the dollar, liquidity and growth expectations.

### Base path — selective rotation with volatility

This is the scenario I consider most consistent with evidence available at October’s close.

Corporate earnings keep growing, but substantial sector and company divergence persists.

Small companies and the equal-weight S&P 500 regain some leadership, while technology still needs to demonstrate that valuations and financing needs are justified.

In this scenario, SPY could trade sideways with volatility episodes and substantial sector differences.

GLD would need to stabilize after its correction; FXI would remain conditional on Chinese economic activity; EWJ would find support in reforms and earnings but face Japanese monetary-policy risks.

For BTC and ETH, stability would depend particularly on recovering flows and liquidity conditions.

### Favorable path — sustained breadth and better financial conditions

This scenario would gain importance if breadth recovery continues and large technology companies stabilize without the rest of the market weakening.

We would also need improved conversion of AI capex into cash flow, stable credit and less real-yield pressure.

For SPY, simultaneous recovery of technology leadership and other companies’ participation would be favorable.

FXI would need improving growth and business expectations; EWJ would need a favorable combination of earnings, reforms and monetary stability.

For BTC and ETH, recovering ETF inflows and normalizing liquidity would be important signals. For Ethereum, I also want institutional adoption to translate into greater economic demand for the token.

### Adverse path — financial deterioration and deleveraging

The main risk would be technology weakness spreading to the rest of the market.

Persistent inflation, higher financing costs, deteriorating credit and earnings below expectations could jointly increase valuation pressure.

In that scenario, S&P 500 concentration could amplify declines.

Gold would not necessarily provide an immediate haven: financial stress can also trigger selling to obtain liquidity.

China would remain exposed to economic weakness and the dollar, while Japan would face possible yen, government-bond and international-financing tensions.

Bitcoin and Ethereum could be particularly affected by capital outflows, liquidations and deleveraging.

### Methodology

**Returns by asset**

SPY, GLD, FXI and EWJ changes are calculated from dollar closing prices for October 16 and October 30, 2026.

R = (P(Oct 30) / P(Oct 16) − 1) × 100

Results exclude dividends, transaction costs and tax adjustments.

BTC and ETH use the data provider’s daily closes, which do not necessarily coincide with US market closing times.

**Sector rotation**

The eleven sector ETFs used in the luiguiherrera.com dashboard are analyzed:

XLK, XLF, XLV, XLE, XLY, XLP, XLI, XLB, XLU, XLRE and XLC.

Weekly returns use five complete sessions between October 23 and October 30 closes.

RSP–SPY, IWM–SPY and QQQ–SPY comparisons are return differences, expressed in percentage points.

**Earnings and implied move**

The implied move comes from the midpoint cost of a complete at-the-money straddle, divided by the stock’s reference price.

The realized move corresponds to the first trading session incorporating the earnings release, respecting whether the announcement occurred before the open or after the close.

The implied move is a magnitude expectation derived from option prices. It is not directional and does not guarantee that the actual move stays within the range.

**ETF flows**

Bitcoin and Ethereum flows come from Farside Investors’ daily data.

These should not be confused with all digital-asset inflows or outflows, because other investment and financing channels exist.

**Seasonality**

Historical statistics are interpreted according to the period observed, sample size and return convention used.

Historical success rates do not represent guaranteed probabilities for November.

**Statistical levels**

Statistical Levels remain separate from macroeconomic and institutional interpretations.

Percentiles, standardized deviations and statistical bands require comparable observations and reproducible methodology. They are neither predictive levels nor investment recommendations.
